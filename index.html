Was Australia ever really treated as ‘nobody’s land’?

This case names Torres Strait Islander people who have died, including Eddie Koiki Mabo (died 1992). Teachers should follow the protocols of the Meriam people of Mer, and with AIATSIS guidance and the AIATSIS Code of Ethics before adding further community voices, oral histories or images. No photograph of Eddie Mabo is used here: the pictures that exist are not clearly free to reuse, and images of the deceased are used only with community guidance.

What is agreed

  • Eddie Koiki Mabo was a Meriam man from Mer (Murray Island) in the Torres Strait. In May 1982 he and other Meriam plaintiffs, including David Passi, Sam Passi, Celuia Mapo Salee and James Rice, began a case in the High Court about ownership of land on the Murray Islands.
  • While the case was running, the Queensland Parliament passed the Queensland Coast Islands Declaratory Act 1985. Section 3 declared that, on annexation, the islands “were vested in the Crown in right of Queensland freed from all other rights, interests and claims of any kind whatsoever.” Section 5 said no compensation “was or is payable.”
  • In Mabo v Queensland (No 1), decided 8 December 1988, the High Court held that the 1985 Act was invalid to the extent it was inconsistent with the Racial Discrimination Act 1975 (Cth). The land case could go on.
  • Eddie Mabo died on 21 January 1992. On 3 June 1992, in Mabo v Queensland (No 2), six of the seven High Court justices held that the common law of Australia can recognise native title, and that the Meriam people were entitled, as against the whole world, to the possession, use, occupation and enjoyment of most of the lands of the Murray Islands (subject to some particular Crown interests).
  • Justice Brennan’s leading reasons discussed and rejected what he called an “enlarged notion of terra nullius.” Historians and some lawyers debate when the Latin phrase itself was first used in Australian law, and whether earlier official language used other words for a similar idea. That debate is flagged, not settled, in this file.
  • The Native Title Act 1993 (Cth) followed the decision. This case stops at the 1992 judgment and the words already on the record.

Ask three witnesses

Each card uses recorded words. Ask the same three questions of each witness.

Place and time

    Eddie Koiki Mabo

    Meriam man of Mer (Murray Island) · lead plaintiff · died 21 January 1992, before the High Court decided the land case

    Interviewed on his land at Las, Mer, in 1989, and remembered in later museum and film records.

    Source. The line about his father is quoted by the National Museum of Australia on its Mabo defining-moment page. The longer account of generations at Las is from an interview filmed by Trevor Graham at Las in 1989 (Yarra Bank Films; transcript published via mabonativetitle.com). These are his recorded words, not a speech invented for this file.

    What they said. The National Museum quotes him: “My father told me: ‘Son, this land will belong to you when I die.’” In 1989, on the land at Las, he said: “There were a total of 18 generations before my grandfather’s time that occupied this land and of course, from my grandfather to my father and myself makes it up to 20 generations all together. That we’ve occupied this area.” He described how later, after the London Missionary Society came, people were moved to the front village.

    Could they really have seen it? He was speaking about his own family’s land and Meriam memory, not about a Latin phrase in a law book. He was a plaintiff, not a judge. Some of his particular claims to individual plots were not all accepted in the Queensland fact-finding that sat under the High Court case. That conflict is in the Teacher Note. It does not cancel the words he did say about generations on the land.

    Why might they say it? To show that Meriam people already had a law of land, passed down, before a court in Canberra was asked to recognise it. He was arguing a case, and he was also telling a family history.

    Justice Brennan

    Justice of the High Court of Australia · leading reasons in Mabo (No 2), 3 June 1992

    A written judgment, not a speech from the street.

    Source. Mabo v Queensland (No 2) [1992] HCA 23; (1992) 175 CLR 1, Brennan J. The judgment is on AustLII: http://www.austlii.edu.au/au/cases/cth/HCA/1992/23

    What they said. Brennan J wrote that “the fiction by which the rights and interests of indigenous inhabitants in land were treated as non-existent was justified by a policy which has no place in the contemporary law of this country.” He also wrote: “It is imperative in today’s world that the common law should neither be nor be seen to be frozen in an age of racial discrimination.” He said the common law “would perpetuate injustice if it were to continue to embrace the enlarged notion of terra nullius and persist in characterizing the indigenous inhabitants of the Australian colonies as people too low in the scale of social organization to be acknowledged as possessing rights and interests in land.”

    Could they really have seen it? He was deciding a case in 1992, from evidence and earlier law, including findings made in the Queensland Supreme Court. He was not an eyewitness to 1788, or to life on Mer. A judgment is an official interpretation of the law, written for a reason, by a judge in the majority.

    Why might they say it? To explain why the Court would no longer treat Indigenous people as if they had had no rights in land, and to set out a legal basis for native title. The words “enlarged notion of terra nullius” are his. Whether that Latin phrase was used in those terms in 1788 is a separate historical question, flagged in Super Sleuths.

    The State of Queensland

    Defendant in the Mabo litigation · Parliament of Queensland, 1985

    The government’s case, in the words of a statute passed while the Meriam claim was already before the courts.

    Source. Queensland Coast Islands Declaratory Act 1985 (Qld), assented to 15 April 1985, ss 3 and 5. Official text: https://www.legislation.qld.gov.au/view/pdf/asmade/act-1985-027 The High Court later held the Act invalid, to the extent of inconsistency with the Racial Discrimination Act, in Mabo (No 1) [1988] HCA 69.

    What they said. The long title was “An Act to allay any doubts that may exist concerning certain islands forming part of Queensland.” Section 3 declared that, on annexation, “the islands were vested in the Crown in right of Queensland freed from all other rights, interests and claims of any kind whatsoever and became waste lands of the Crown.” Section 5 declared: “No compensation was or is payable to any person” by reason of the annexation, or in respect of any right, interest or claim alleged to have existed before it, or by reason of any later disposal.

    Could they really have seen it? This is not one person’s memory. It is a Parliament changing, or trying to declare, the legal consequences of annexation in 1879. The High Court in 1988 treated the declarations as an attempt to extinguish the very rights the Meriam plaintiffs were claiming. A statute can tell you what a government wanted the law to be. It cannot, by itself, tell you whether Meriam people had already been living under their own land law.

    Why might they say it? The Act was passed while the case was on foot. The High Court later found that, had it stood, it would have extinguished the claimed traditional rights. Students may fairly ask whether “allaying doubts” was also an attempt to end the case. The 1988 judgment is the place to test that, not a guess in the margin.

    My finding

    The three witness cards are Eddie Koiki Mabo, Justice Brennan, and the State of Queensland. Further questions are in Super Sleuths. There is no answer at the back.

    Was Australia ever really treated as ‘nobody’s land’?

    Write a finding. You may use one of these, or another wording of your own: Yes — later law treated Indigenous land rights as if they were not there · The idea was used, even if the Latin phrase came later · The Court in 1992 rejected that treatment, but historians still argue about the words · We can’t know, from this file, exactly how people in 1788 would have put it · Another finding.

    Why it still matters

    The 1992 decision is still the starting point for native title in Australian law. It does not give every First Nations group a title, and it did not undo every dispossession. It did say that the common law can recognise rights that already existed under Indigenous law and custom, where they have not been extinguished. Meriam people were the plaintiffs. The principle was then used more widely. Debates about the phrase terra nullius, about what the Court did and did not do, and about unfinished business after 1993, are still live. A careful finding will not pretend the argument is over.

    Eddie Koiki Mabo

    Background. A Meriam man from Mer. Lived for many years in Townsville. The case grew from conversations about whether Meriam people owned their land under Australian law.

    How he came to the place. He was from Mer. The 1989 interview used on the witness card was filmed at Las, on the island.

    How he came to the place. By sitting on the appeal, after fact-finding in Queensland.

    Movements before the incident. The written reasons follow argument and the 1988 decision in Mabo (No 1).

    The incident. The publication of reasons on 3 June 1992.

    After the incident. Parliament passed the Native Title Act 1993. Later cases (including Wik) are outside this file.

    Background. The State was the defendant. In 1985 its Parliament passed a declaratory Act about the annexed islands.

    How it came to the place. As the government of the State that had annexed the islands in 1879, and as the party being sued.

    Movements before the incident. The Meriam claim had already been filed in 1982.

    The incident. The 1985 Act; then the 1988 High Court decision striking at it.

    After the incident. The land case continued, and was decided against the State’s broad position in 1992, Dawson J dissenting.

    Evidence for and against, and the phrase debate

    For the idea that Australia was treated as nobody’s land. Brennan J describes a “fiction” that treated Indigenous land rights as non-existent, and an “enlarged notion of terra nullius.” The 1985 Queensland Act tried to declare the islands vested in the Crown, “freed from all other rights,” with no compensation. For a long time Australian law, after cases such as Milirrpum v Nabalco (1971), did not recognise native title.

    Against treating the Latin phrase as an official 1788 slogan. Historians and some lawyers — including David Ritter, “The ‘Rejection of Terra Nullius’ in Mabo: A Critical Analysis,” (1996) 18 Sydney Law Review 5, and work by Merete Borch and Bain Attwood — argue that the exact phrase was not the everyday legal formula of 1788, and that Mabo’s “rejection of terra nullius” names a later way of describing a pattern of thought. Earlier official language used other words, such as “waste lands,” or ideas about “settled” as against “conquered” colonies.

    What this file does not do. It does not tell students to pick a side and then hide the other. The question is whether Australia was treated as nobody’s land — in law and policy — not whether every official in 1788 used those two Latin words.

    What we still can’t know
    • Exactly when the phrase terra nullius first entered Australian legal usage, in the form later discussed in Mabo. Scholars disagree.
    • How Eddie Mabo would have answered the judgment of 3 June 1992. He had died.
    • Every Meriam person’s view of the case. This file uses the lead plaintiff’s recorded words and the Court’s orders about Meriam title, not a single “community voice” invented for the page.
    Sure / maybe / guess

    Sure, from this file. The 1982 claim; the 1985 Act’s wording; Mabo (No 1) in 1988; Eddie Mabo’s death on 21 January 1992; the 3 June 1992 majority decision; Brennan J’s published sentences quoted on the card; the 1989 interview at Las.

    Maybe. How far “terra nullius” as a phrase, rather than as a later label for a set of ideas, organised official thinking in the late eighteenth century.

    Guess — do not write these as fact. What Eddie Mabo would have said on 3 June 1992. That every historian now agrees about the phrase. That the 1992 decision “gave the land back” in a simple way to all First Nations people.
